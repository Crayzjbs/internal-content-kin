# KIN VOICE GUIDE

Status: DRAFT, waiting for Justine's approval.

This guide covers how KIN sounds: the narration the voice actor reads, the on-screen text, and the copy around each video (titles, descriptions, captions, replies). The series bible says what we make. This guide says how we say it.

## The KIN narrator
- A sharp friend who did all the research and tells you the story over dinner.
- Curious, clear, and calm. Never shouting, never breathless.
- Confident about facts, honest about what is unknown.
- Fair to everyone in the story, including the people who messed up.
- Has opinions, labels them as opinions, and earns them with facts first.

## Tone in one line
Clear like a teacher, gripping like a storyteller, fair like a judge.

## Tone by pillar
| Pillar | Lean into | Avoid |
| Mysteries | Quiet tension, careful wording, letting a strange detail sit | Spooky voice, fake suspense, treating theories as fact |
| Rise and fall | Momentum, irony, the human side of the fall | Gloating, piling on, mocking the person |
| Business stories | Clarity, numbers made simple, "here is how it actually worked" | Jargon, lecture tone, hype about money |

## Sentence rules
- Short sentences. Most under 15 words. One idea per sentence.
- Write how people talk. Contractions are fine: "it's", "didn't", "they'd".
- Read every line aloud. If you run out of breath or trip on it, rewrite it.
- Use specifics: names, dates, places, amounts. "In March 2019, the company had 40 employees" beats "the company was growing fast".
- Explain anything a global viewer might not know: local brands, currencies, slang, laws. Give money in its original currency and US dollars.
- Use numbers the ear can hold. Round when the exact figure does not matter ("about 3 million"), keep it exact when it does.
- Vary rhythm. After a long sentence, hit with a short one.
- No em dashes. Use commas, colons, or periods.

## How we build tension
- Ask the question before giving the answer. Let the viewer wonder for a beat.
- Use contrast: what people believed, then what was true.
- End chapters on an open question or a turn, not a summary.
- Reveal, then react. State the fact plainly, then one line on why it matters.
- Never fake it. If the answer is boring, say so and move on. Fake suspense breaks trust.

## How we talk about real people
- Allegations are always attributed: "according to the lawsuit", "former employees claimed", "police said".
- Anyone not convicted is "accused" or "suspect".
- Always include their side: a response, an apology, a denial, or "did not respond to requests for comment" when that is true.
- Criticize actions and decisions, never looks, mental health, family, or private life that is not part of the story.
- When someone was wronged, say so clearly. Fair does not mean neutral about harm.

## Opinion and commentary
- Facts first, opinion second, and the opinion is marked: "In my view", "Honestly, this is where it falls apart", "That's a choice".
- One sharp line of commentary lands harder than a paragraph of it.
- Light, dry humor is welcome in rise and fall and business stories. Never joke about victims, deaths, or serious harm.

## Words and phrases we do not use
- Filler openings: "Hey guys", "Welcome back", "In this video", "Before we start, make sure to subscribe".
- Fake suspense: "You won't believe", "What happened next will shock you", "Little did they know".
- Hype words: revolutionary, game changer, unlock, elevate, next level, skyrocket, insane, crazy (as filler).
- Certainty we do not have: "obviously", "clearly guilty", "everyone knows".
- Unattributed blame: "the scammer", "the killer", "the fraud" for anyone not convicted.

## Examples
These show the pattern. Names in brackets are placeholders.

Cold open
- Weak: "Today we're going to talk about [Company], a company that had a really interesting story."
- Strong: "In 2021, [Company] was worth 4 billion dollars. Eighteen months later, it was worth nothing. And the warning signs were posted on its own website."

Stating an allegation
- Weak: "[Name] stole millions from his investors."
- Strong: "The lawsuit claims [Name] moved 3 million dollars of investor money into his own accounts. He denies it, and the case is still open."

Commentary
- Weak: "This was a really, really bad decision that made no sense at all."
- Strong: "So with sales falling, they doubled the ad budget. That's a choice."

Mystery tension
- Weak: "And then something super creepy and terrifying happened."
- Strong: "The door was locked from the inside. The window was painted shut. And the room was empty."

## Narration notes for the voice actor
- Pace: about 150 words per minute. Slow down for key facts and reveals.
- Mark pauses as [beat] and tone shifts as [slower], [lighter], or [serious] in vo-script.md.
- Names, places, and foreign words come with a pronunciation guide. If a name has more than one pronunciation, use the one the person or place uses.
- Warm but not chatty. Serious but not dark.

## Copy voice (titles, descriptions, captions, replies)
- Same narrator, shorter breath.
- Titles: a name plus a tension, as in the series bible. Curious, never false.
- Descriptions: plain and useful. Hook line, short summary, chapters, sources, credits.
- Captions: hook first, one idea, one CTA.
- Replies: warm, short, human. Thank people, answer real questions, never argue.
